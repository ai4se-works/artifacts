\begin{abstract}
AI for Software Engineering (AI4SE) uses AI in software-engineering work.
Here, ``mid-to-large'' means more than 200 R\&D staff; two client cases,
Org-A and Org-B, each have more than 1,000 R\&D staff, multi-site delivery,
brownfield systems, and established processes. In this setting, heterogeneous
AI practice creates verification and transfer risk, while sponsors may mistake
a pilot for scale readiness. Existing staging vocabulary does not
specify how to separate pilot-method closeout from wave-specific Scale
admission. This study addresses that problem by reporting an evidence-based
transformation framework and method organized as an
Explore--Consolidation--conditional-Scale path, with evidence reviews.
Results show that, at Org-A, a six-week Explore produced citable
method, opportunity assets, and a readiness-labeled executable ledger;
management passed Explore closeout but withheld Scale admission. In an observed
five-week Consolidation slice, the team recorded 15 requirements, 18 changes,
and 8 finished changes. All 18 changes were processed through the
Spec-Driven Development (SDD) workflow, with a corresponding specification
produced for each change; Verify, Align, and role gaps kept Scale conditional.
After judging alternative AI4SE
approaches infeasible for its setting, Org-B selected the path instantiated at
Org-A and began adapting it to incumbent IPD and SAFe-class constraints. Its
record is a briefing-level adaptation design, not evidence of formal
closeout, later-stage delivery, or an ART/Portfolio rewrite. Findings
support a bounded feasibility argument with unequal evidence strength: Org-A
supplies observed method-chain and Consolidation evidence, while Org-B supplies
an adaptation design. The contribution separates pilot closeout from
conditional Scale admission: completion can trigger evidence review and
support Consolidation, but not Scale without transfer evidence. We do not
claim organization-wide Scale, completed Scale admission, or
platform-measured return.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% EVIDENCE: ../outline.md §I; design spec §3

In this paper, ``mid-to-large'' means an organization with more than 200 R\&D
staff. The two client organizations examined here, Org-A and Org-B, each have
more than 1,000 R\&D staff, operate
across multiple delivery sites, maintain substantial brownfield systems, and
rely on established, relatively conventional engineering processes. In these
mid-to-large engineering organizations, AI assistants are adopted unevenly
across tools, prompts, specification habits, and human--agent handoffs.
Heterogeneity raises organizational entropy: process risk grows, quality is
hard to underwrite when verification and intent ownership fragment, and
key-person departure removes tacit ``AI-fluent'' know-how that never entered a
citable method.
Enterprise software engineering must therefore pursue \emph{trustworthiness}
and \emph{sustainability}, not throughput slogans alone~\cite{Forsgren2021SPACE}.
The motive continues earlier industrial process change: large manufacturing
organizations already used process-and-quality transformations such as
Integrated Product Development (IPD) to keep complex integration credible;
under AI, AI4SE is the analogous upgrade for human--agent delivery---speed
subordinated to governable evidence.
The paper targets complex-delivery organizations: small innovative firms may
prioritize speed over heavy gates, whereas organization-wide ``AI acceleration''
requires management capacity and maturity that many incumbents lack.

Sponsors still err by equating assistant access, or a facilitated pilot, with
scale-ready capability.
Neither shows that a new team can run a complete cycle under agreed
guardrails.
AI assistance compresses \emph{implementation} cost while amplifying errors
from vague intent, weak specifications, and unverified agent output
(\emph{definition scarcity} and verification capacity).
Treating a well-received pilot as organization-wide diffusion authorization is
the failure mode this paper records.

Five recurring AI4SE failure modes (DevOps patterns reframed for AI) also
shape the design:
\emph{value-curve misread} (underestimating the transformation J-curve:
early gains can be followed by a temporary efficiency dip as teams absorb new
practices, add testing, and pay down technical debt~\cite{DORA2018Accelerate};
in AI4SE, learning to validate and repair model output can deepen that dip);
\emph{no pilot capacity} under delivery pressure (fragmentary tool trials);
\emph{tool-procurement equivalence} without redesigning review/security/compliance
for generated artifacts;
\emph{external-consultant dependence} without developing internal coaches who
understand both software engineering and model limitations; and
\emph{metric mismatch} (LOC/hours/speed rewarding unreviewed AI paste-ins).
The path counters them with evidence-based admission, method-before-tool,
teachable ownership, and measurement on a companion assessment track.

The unresolved bottleneck is therefore not whether AI can assist one team, but
how an organization can decide when a locally feasible method has become
transferable to another team.

We propose and report an evidence-based AI4SE transformation framework and
method. Its \emph{path} is Explore--Consolidation--Scale; its
\emph{admission contract} specifies stage-boundary evidence; and its
\emph{operating stack} names the delivery vocabulary carried by the path.
Relative to classical Stage-Gate business-case gates~\cite{Cooper1990StageGate},
admission inspects a citable method, a stub-versus-ready ledger, and transfer
preconditions. Each stage has a purpose, work products, and an observable exit
that tests entry to the next: Explore asks whether a method runs end-to-end,
Consolidation whether the pilot team can repeat and teach it, and conditional
Scale whether a new team can run a cycle independently.
Org-A supplies the primary longitudinal evidence.  After Org-B judged
alternative AI4SE transformation approaches infeasible for its setting, it
selected the transformation path instantiated at Org-A and began adapting it
to its IPD and SAFe-class constraints; the available record is a
briefing-level late-Explore adaptation design.
The transferable artifact is therefore the framework's path-plus-admission
contract, not a tool catalog or completed rollout; operating-stack terms name
the practices carried by that contract.

We organize the experience around three questions.  The questions are stated
at the level of the framework and its evidence; the cases provide the
observations and adaptation record:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Q1.}
What evidence-based framework and method can structure an industrial AI4SE
transformation from pilot exploration toward conditional scale, so that
calendar completion is not treated as scale readiness?
(Answered in Section~\ref{sec:path}.)
\item \textbf{Q2.}
What evidence did Org-A's completed Explore produce, and why did that evidence
support Explore closeout and entry into Consolidation without supporting Scale
admission?
(Answered in Section~\ref{sec:org-a}.)
\item \textbf{Q3.}
What team-level adaptation design was documented for the SDD-centered
framework in late Explore under incumbent IPD and SAFe-class constraints, and
what evidence boundaries remained?
(Answered in Sections~\ref{sec:org-b} and~\ref{sec:discussion}.)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Methodological stance.}
This manuscript is an industrial experience report~\cite{Runeson2009CaseStudy}.
Facilitators co-designed the path, but the defended claims are Org-A's
management decisions (Explore closeout met / Scale admission held) and the
readiness labels recorded at the executive readout, not a consultant self-score.
Org-B is a secondary late-Explore briefing case with no intranet mirror.
We do not claim theoretical saturation, a completed DSR evaluate
cycle~\cite{Hevner2004DSR}, researcher-owned action-research agency, or
completed Scale at either organization.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(1)~an evidence-based framework and method for mid-to-large organizations,
with an Explore--Consolidation--Scale path, stage work products, a
citable/runnable/transferable admission contract, and a compact operating
stack; (2)~bounded Org-A feasibility evidence---Explore closeout, an observed
client-operated Consolidation midpoint, and withheld Scale admission; and
(3)~a briefing-level Org-B account of team-level adaptation under harder IPD
plus SAFe-class constraints, without evidence of formal closeout,
Consolidation, or Scale admission. Both organizations plan subsequent scale
work, but that plan is not organization-wide Scale. Explore effectiveness and
maturity signals are bounded stage inputs; their quantitative stratification
belongs to a companion manuscript and is not reproduced here.

\subsection{Domain Fit: Why Exploration Is Not Instant Scale}
\label{sec:cynefin}

Snowden and Boone's Cynefin framing distinguishes decision domains in which
best practice, expert analysis, or probe-and-sense behavior is
appropriate~\cite{Snowden2007Cynefin}.
Early industrial AI4SE work is typically \emph{complex}: workable method is
emergent, so Explore is a probe that must produce inspectable method assets
rather than a purchased best-practice pack.
Consolidation moves durable findings toward the \emph{complicated} domain:
playbooks, harness hardening, and seed coaches support sense--analyze--respond
transfer.
Scale entry is admitted only when enough of the method is stable to diffuse
without re-running full discovery---closer to clear or well-analyzed
complicated work, never by calendar alone.
The gates therefore match the decision to the nature of the work.  A method
still being explored in the Complex domain should not be treated as ready for
organization-wide rollout.  The failure mode recorded here is pilot success
without Scale admission.

\subsection{Industry Fault Lines as Staging Rationale}
\label{sec:tw-retreats}

Senior-practitioner syntheses from Thoughtworks' Utah and Europe Future of
Software Engineering retreats highlight fault lines that motivate
staging~\cite{Thoughtworks2026UtahRetreat,Thoughtworks2026EuropeRetreat}:
engineering rigor migrating into specifications, tests, and constraints; a
supervisory \emph{middle loop} between coding and delivery; verification
(not generation) as scarce capacity; harness ownership around the agent; and
a two-clock mismatch in which code-production time collapses while decision
time does not.
We use that map only as industrial \emph{rationale}: Explore freezes probe
evidence; Consolidation hardens verification, harness ownership, and seed
coaches; Scale waits on decision/verification capacity for a receiving team.
Retreat anecdotes are not Org-A/Org-B facts
(Section~\ref{sec:related-work}).

\subsection{Layer Inheritance: Pressman Structure, Upgraded Semantics}
\label{sec:pressman}

Classical software-engineering textbooks present a layered view in which a
quality focus grounds process, methods, and tools, and in which more visible
layers depend on less visible ones~\cite{Pressman2014SEPA}.
Figure~\ref{fig:pressman-ai4se} states our industrial reading: the
\emph{structure} is inherited; the \emph{semantics} must be upgraded for
human--agent delivery.
Quality focus becomes \emph{Effectiveness} (quality---including
\emph{trust}---plus waste-aware efficiency that serves \emph{value}, not
speed alone).
Process, methods, and tools retain their roles, but executors are humans
\emph{and} agents.
Harness Engineering, Spec-Driven Development (SDD), and Agentic Engineering
are placed in the \emph{methods} family (discipline and assets), not as a
replacement for the process layer or as a tools-only purchase.
\emph{Harmony} is a cross-cutting human--agent contract, not a fifth stacked
layer.

\subsection{Operating Stack: Value Layer, Team Paradigm, and SDD}
\label{sec:operating-stack}

Pressman's layers answer \emph{what} to upgrade; delivery teams still need an
\emph{operating stack} that says how Lean/Agile work continues under AI4SE.
Figure~\ref{fig:operating-stack} states three nested layers we used with
sponsors and coaches.
The top layer keeps Lean/Agile value principles (flow, pull, feedback, respect
for people).
The middle layer keeps classical team operating modes---Scrum, Kanban, or
Scrumban---as the cadence and role container (Org-A primarily Scrum; Org-B
combines IPD and SAFe-class scaled-agile constraints with multiple team modes).
The bottom layer is Spec-Driven Development (SDD): Align, Verify, and Merge
reviews that protect each change under the chosen paradigm \(p\).

The industrial claim is deliberately narrow: those team modes remain
effective; what shifts is the primary \emph{unit of value flow}.
Where boards and backlogs once centered on user stories, SDD centers on a
citable \emph{change request} (and its Align$\rightarrow$Verify$\rightarrow$Merge
contract) as the object that carries definition and verification evidence.
Stories and other backlog items may still exist; they no longer substitute for
an inspectable change contract when agents draft and apply increments.
As implementation cost falls, definition and verification become the scarce
steps within the same inspect--adapt container (a gradual shift toward
change-request flow, not a discard of Scrum cadence).
We reserve the word \emph{gate} for those process-level Align/Verify/Merge
reviews in the method pack, and we use \emph{closeout} / \emph{admission} for
organizational stage decisions in Section~\ref{sec:path}---so the two loops
do not share G1/G2 numbering.

\subsection{Effectiveness, Harmony, and Four In-Path Directions}

We use two cross-cutting concerns---\emph{Effectiveness} and
\emph{Harmony}---and four stage-relative directions (SDD, Agentic Engineering,
Harness Engineering, Operating Model) as path vocabulary, not as a maturity
model or a substitute for stage closeout and admission.
Effectiveness requires inspectable evidence that selected work ran under local
constraints (citable method, validation-vehicle run-through, or new-team
cycle) rather than attendance or license counts; quality includes
\emph{trust} (permissions, safety, compliance), and efficiency means valuable
outcomes without waste.
Harmony requires operationalized human--agent accountability under four named
functions---\emph{Intent Owner}, \emph{Spec Steward} (specification architect),
\emph{AI Orchestrator}, and \emph{Verification Lead}.
These functions may be combined but must not be vacant, and
\emph{contract separation} forbids the apply party from being the sole
verifier.
As enablement heuristics (not Org-A/Org-B measured protocols)\label{sec:team-topology},
formation change stays \emph{minimal}---keep Scrum-class size/cadence;
subdivide into cells with Spec Steward + Verification Lead; share Intent Owner
and AI Orchestrator across cells when Intent Owner is not sole verifier and
Orchestrator does not self-certify apply---and risk routing uses
human-in-the-loop (HITL) for plan/review/ship, human-on-the-loop (HOTL) for
supervised execution, and human-out-of-the-loop (HOOL) only for bounded
low-risk drafts.
Both concerns feed Explore closeout and Scale admission criteria as design
vocabulary; they are not reported here as measured organizational return.

Along the path: \textbf{SDD} yields a citable then teachable specification
contract;
\textbf{Agentic} work must become a repeatable main path under guardrails;
\textbf{Harness} moves from disclosed scaffold to hardened starter kit (a stub
is not production-ready; Section~\ref{sec:org-a});
\textbf{Operating Model} names owners and seed coaches so a hardened harness
has an authorized receiving team (role-cell heuristics above remain optional
design aids).
Recent SDD/agentic writing~\cite{Gupta2026SDDAgentic} is peer thematic context
only; it does not supply Org-A stage evidence.

\subsection{Certainty Boundary: User Harness, Model, and Method Assets}
\label{sec:harness-boundary}

Industrial harness writing treats the coding-agent harness as the non-model
layer (guides, sensors, and user-owned outer controls) in a bounded
context~\cite{Boeckeler2026Harness}.
Figure~\ref{fig:harness-certainty} is our AI4SE reading: organizational
certainty does not live in the model alone.
A governing delivery paradigm \(p\) (for example SDD versus loosely guided
``vibe coding'') selects outer user-harness and coding-agent contracts around
a comparatively stable model; Explore must freeze inspectable structure and
Consolidation must harden ownership.
Tool design is \emph{process-shaped}: AI assets (commands, agents, skills,
rules, hooks) package against Align/Verify/Merge and stage flows under a User
Harness Framework (core workflow $\leftrightarrow$ user-asset pack), not a
post-hoc tool catalog; \emph{agentic engineering} composes them into a
governed main path, with HITL/HOTL/HOOL duty inside asset design.
Method assets feed the harness; they do not replace the model.
The harness remains an open SDD-stack class (OpenSpec / Superpowers /
Spec-Kit style examples, and peers)---a constraint Org-B makes explicit in
Section~\ref{sec:org-b}.

\subsection{Why Closeout and Admission, Not a Calendar, Advance the Path}

We define an AI4SE transformation path as a sequence of capability-building
stages separated by explicit management decisions.  A stage has a purpose,
work products, and an observable exit condition.  Its exit condition is also
an admission test for the next stage: elapsed time can trigger a review, but
cannot substitute for evidence.  This distinction is important because a
pilot can finish on schedule while leaving its method tacit, its automation
fragile, or its ownership unresolved.
Collapsing code-production time does not by itself collapse decision and
verification time---another reason reviews must examine evidence rather than
calendars.

The transformation path nests two control loops.
\emph{Stage closeout and admission decisions} decide whether the organization may enter
Consolidation or admit a Scale wave.
Inside delivery work, shorter \emph{Align/Verify/Merge} reviews---align a delta
specification, verify a candidate increment, then merge and
archive---protect each change under the chosen paradigm \(p\).
Stage criteria ask whether method, assets, and ownership are transferable;
Align/Verify/Merge reviews ask whether a particular change is definition-complete and
evidence-backed.
Confusing the two is a common failure: a team that passes local change
reviews can still fail Scale admission if receiving teams cannot inherit the method.
(Process vocabulary only: Align/Verify/Merge sit mainly in local
coding/harness loops; stage closeout/admission remain outer governance.)

\subsection{Stages, Deliverables, and Exit Criteria}

% FACT-III-B
\textbf{Stage 1---Explore.}
The purpose of Explore is feasibility, not broad deployment.  In the Org-A
instantiation, the indicative duration was approximately six weeks, spanning
diagnosis and scenario selection, four workshop weeks over twelve opportunity
themes, co-creation of an initial playbook and executable-asset scaffold, and
use of a pilot change request as a validation vehicle.  The exit criterion
was an end-to-end run-through of the method chain.  It was deliberately not
completion of every feature in the validation vehicle.

% FACT-III-C
\textbf{Stage 2---Consolidation.}
Consolidation is the bridge between one successful run and a transferable
organizational capability.  Its indicative Org-A window was one to two
months.  The work comprises revising clauses that read correctly but do not
run in practice, hardening the principal executable path from stubs to usable
assets, assigning role ownership, and rehearsing coach-the-coach transfer.
Its intended products are a standardized playbook v2, a hardened toolset, and
seed coaches.
Hardening follows two practical rules drawn from the enablement practice:
\emph{do not promote immature automation}, and \emph{keep apply separate
from verify}.
As a Consolidation heuristic we refuse to skip
rule~$\rightarrow$skill~$\rightarrow$agent~$\rightarrow$command~$\rightarrow$hook
promotion; we do not treat reuse/hit/drift/retire rates as ROI here.
The exit condition is ``team can teach'': pilot-team members can explain and
transfer the method, rather than only reproduce a consultant-led
demonstration.

% FACT-III-D
\textbf{Stage 3---Scale.}
Scale is quality-consistent, wave-based diffusion rather than simultaneous
organization-wide rollout.  Admission requires the Consolidation exit,
named role owners, a starter kit for sibling teams, and reserved sponsor
cadence and resources.  Seed coaches then enable successive teams and review
practice consistency.  The terminal evidence is a fresh team's independent
completion of a cycle under the guardrails.  Thus, a calendar target can
prompt a Scale-admission review, but unmet criteria block admission.

\subsection{Closeout and Admission Semantics}

% FACT-III-E
Explore closeout is a \emph{method--asset closeout}.  Management asks whether a playbook can be
shown and cited with its gaps visible; whether opportunity-point
specifications and best practices can be inventoried; whether executable
assets are distinguished as ready or stub; whether effectiveness
and maturity signals carry their measurement boundaries; and whether the
Consolidation-to-Scale path is concrete enough to discuss.  A positive Explore closeout
therefore licenses method hardening, not broad rollout.  Table
\ref{tab:stage-criteria} makes this decision contract explicit.

Scale admission is stricter because it concerns transfer beyond the pilot setting.  It
tests whether know-how has moved from individuals into teachable guidance,
executable assets, role ownership, and an enablement mechanism---and whether
verification authority remains human-held under the nested Align/Verify/Merge reviews.
The resulting admission decision is reversible and wave-specific: if a new
team cannot operate with the starter kit and bounded coaching, the
organization returns the deficient asset or role arrangement to
Consolidation.
This interpretation makes a stage decision a learning control, rather than a ceremonial
approval checkpoint.

\subsection{Operating Stage Criteria as an Evidence Review}

A usable stage review needs a review packet, a decision owner, and a recorded
disposition.  The review packet should present the stage goal first and then
map each promised output to an inspectable artifact or observation.  A
readiness label accompanies each item so that ``exists,'' ``runs on the
primary path,'' and ``transferable to a new team'' cannot collapse into one
category.  Known gaps remain in the packet as decision inputs.  Removing
them from view would make an approval easier to obtain but less useful for
planning the next stage.

The decision owner evaluates sufficiency for the \emph{next} stage, not
perfection in the current one.  At Explore closeout, an incomplete executable scaffold can
be acceptable when it is disclosed and when the Consolidation plan names the
hardening work.  At Scale admission, the same scaffold is a blocker because a receiving
team would depend on it.  This relative interpretation prevents two common
errors: rejecting useful Explore evidence because every asset is not yet
finished, and admitting Scale because unfinished assets were tolerated at
Explore closeout.

A stage disposition has three possible forms.  \emph{Advance} means the exit
condition is met and the next stage may start under its declared scope.
\emph{Advance with conditions} records a narrow action whose completion is
checked before a dependent activity begins; it must not be used to waive a
core exit condition.  \emph{Hold} returns named gaps to the current stage.
In all three forms, the evidence and rationale remain available for the next
review.  This creates continuity across decisions and makes negative
evidence---for example, a stubbed primary path or missing owner---actionable
rather than embarrassing.

The review also separates two kinds of uncertainty.  Product uncertainty asks
whether AI4SE can improve the selected work under local constraints; Explore
reduces it through actual method use.  Transfer uncertainty asks whether
another team can reproduce that use without recreating the method; only
Consolidation and a new-team test can reduce it.  Treating these uncertainties
separately explains why a positive Explore closeout and a negative Scale admission can be the expected,
internally consistent outcome of the same review.

\subsection{Three-Claim Path-Progression Argument}

The path permits a useful feasibility judgment before claiming completed
diffusion.  We structure that judgment as three claims (not process L0/L1
levels, and not the Method--Evidence asset classes).

\textbf{Claim 1---the path is designed.}
Stage goals, work products, exits, and admissions are stated before scale readiness
is assessed.  This makes the claim falsifiable: missing ownership, hardening,
or transfer evidence can stop advancement even when a pilot was well
received.

% FACT-III-F
\textbf{Claim 2---Explore is observed end-to-end.}
For Org-A, the initial playbook, opportunity assets, and validation vehicle
exercised the method chain under real delivery constraints.  The evidence
supports ``can run once'' and justified meeting Explore closeout; it does not establish
repeatability across teams.

% FACT-III-G
\textbf{Claim 3---Scale remains conditional.}
Org-A's pilot succeeded at Explore, but Scale admission was not met at closeout.
Consolidation---including asset hardening and team self-solve
capability---is the prerequisite for a later Scale decision.  The feasibility
judgment therefore rests on an explicit path, observed end-to-end use, a
readiness-labeled asset ledger, and testable admission conditions.  It does
not rest on an assumed organization-wide return or on diffusion that has not
occurred.

\subsection{Anti-Patterns Exposed by the Criteria}

\textbf{Tool-only transformation} treats access to a coding assistant or
plugin catalog as the change itself.  It omits the specifications,
verification, context, roles, and learning routines needed to make tool use
repeatable.  Explore closeout exposes this weakness by requiring a citable method and
bounded evidence; Scale admission exposes it by requiring transfer and ownership.

\textbf{Pilot equals scale} converts a successful demonstration directly
into rollout authorization.  It conflates end-to-end feasibility with
cross-team replicability.  Separating Explore closeout from Scale admission preserves the useful result
of the pilot while making the missing admission evidence visible.

\textbf{Skipping Consolidation} carries first-generation prose and stubs
directly into new teams.  The likely burden then moves from explicit
hardening to repeated local improvisation.  The Stage-2 exit---team can
teach---requires the organization to remove that burden before opening a
Scale wave.

\textbf{Agent self-certification} lets the same generative path that applied
a change also declare the change verified.  Nested Align/Verify/Merge reviews and the
Harmony verification function exist to forbid that shortcut: drafts and
execution may be agentic; sign-off remains human.

\subsection{From Activities to Stage Evidence}

The workshop sequence was not treated as attendance evidence.  Each block
was expected to leave a trace in one or more asset layers.  Foundation work
informed the method guidance and executable scaffold; opportunity work
created specifications and best practices; delivery-track use tested whether
those descriptions could guide action; and the closeout evidence pack
recorded both observed signals and their limits.  This trace converted a
series of facilitated sessions into inspectable inputs to Explore closeout.

The parallel tracks in W4 were particularly important to that conversion.
Playbook authoring alone could have produced guidance that was internally
coherent but detached from delivery.  Delivery work alone could have
produced a one-off result whose method remained tacit.  Running authoring and
specification validation together created a feedback loop: delivery exposed
where wording or executable support was weak, while the playbook gave the
team a stable object to revise and later cite.  The closeout review then assessed the
resulting artifacts and disclosed gaps rather than inferring capability from
workshop completion.

We used three readiness distinctions when interpreting the handoff.
\emph{Citable} means an artifact is stable enough to identify and discuss.
\emph{Runnable} means that its principal path works in the intended local
workflow.  \emph{Transferable} adds the ability of another team to use that
path with declared guardrails and bounded coaching.  Explore required a
citable method and evidence of local use; Scale admission would require
runnable and transferable support.  The distinctions explain why the Method-layer
playbook could satisfy its Explore-closeout criterion while the largely stubbed Executable pack
could not satisfy Scale admission.

\subsection{Phase-One Explore Closeout Decision}

At the Explore closeout executive readout, management applied five questions: whether

closeout and admission decisions, management would have had to label the pilot
either a failure because it was not ready for broad diffusion, or a scale
success because it completed its planned activities.  The stage-path account
preserves the observed Explore achievement and the unresolved admission work
at the same time.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Reading the Two Cases Together}

Org-A and Org-B occupy different positions on the same framework path
(Table~\ref{tab:a-b-contrast}) with unequal evidence depth: Org-A is the
primary case (Explore done; Consolidation midpoint; Scale held; in-repo
ledger), whereas Org-B is a briefing-level, harder-fit late-Explore
adaptation (IPD + SAFe L1; P1 bridge), not a second completed Consolidation
packet or Scale admission.

Org-A passed Explore closeout because the method chain had been exercised in
real delivery, a citable playbook and opportunity inventory were available,
and the ledger made remaining executable gaps explicit.  The same evidence
could not support Scale admission because the main executable chain, teachable
ownership, starter kit, and seed-coach conditions were not yet in place.  The
two decisions therefore answer different uncertainty questions rather than
contradicting one another.

The Consolidation midpoint adds process evidence without changing that
boundary.  The five-week slice shows repeated use of selected practices and
mandatory \texttt{/sdd} entry, while the missing Reject/Conditional Verify
records, slower Align review, and unfinished orchestration role identify work
that still belongs in Consolidation.  For Org-B, the IPD-to-SDD bridge is the
corresponding adaptation layer: it changes how the method enters the local
process, but the briefing record does not establish that the full path has
cleared the same closeout or admission tests.

Taken together, Org-A supplies observed method-chain and Consolidation evidence,
while Org-B documents how the P1 bridge was packaged for a harder process
setting. Org-B's method switch does not prove general superiority over unnamed
alternatives, nor does its briefing establish Org-A's ledger or formal
closeout. Both organizations plan subsequent scale work, but that plan is not
organization-wide Scale. The framework targets mid-to-large entropy/trust
settings, and its increment over Stage-Gate business-case gates~\cite{Cooper1990StageGate}
is the AI4SE admission contract: a stubbed chain remains Consolidation work,
Scale can be held after Explore, and harder-fit evidence is not promoted
automatically.

\subsection{Manager Takeaways}

Managers should freeze the process-shaped harness and its paradigm \(p\) on one
stream, require a citable method and readiness ledger before tool-only rollout,
and keep unmet Scale items visible before a second wave. Before Scale, they
should require named owners, seed coaches, a runnable starter kit, and removal
of stub or apply-path self-certification clauses. These are design
implications, not case-validated prerequisites.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

We follow case-study guidance~\cite{Runeson2009CaseStudy} and treat the path as
a designed enablement artifact~\cite{Hevner2004DSR} at Org-A, not saturation
or a completed DSR evaluate cycle. SPACE~\cite{Forsgren2021SPACE} supports
refusing pilot slogans as Scale authorization; maturity measurement is the
companion unit.
Cynefin~\cite{Snowden2007Cynefin}, SE layering~\cite{Pressman2014SEPA},
harness writing~\cite{Boeckeler2026Harness}, and Thoughtworks retreat
taxonomies~\cite{Thoughtworks2026UtahRetreat,Thoughtworks2026EuropeRetreat}
are staging vocabulary only; SDD/agentic writing~\cite{Gupta2026SDDAgentic} is
peer context; AI-assisted maturity models~\cite{Tereci2026AASD} theorize
maturity, while our unit is a path with closeout/admission criteria against
Stage-Gate business-case gates~\cite{Cooper1990StageGate}.
Together, these sources motivate staged and evidence-aware governance, but they
do not specify the transfer contract that separates pilot closeout from later,
wave-specific Scale admission in a brownfield organization with established
processes. The gap addressed here is therefore the absence of an explicit
path-and-admission contract for that transfer decision. We specify the path and
contract, trace the strongest evidence to Org-A, and treat Org-B as a
briefing-level adaptation design rather than a second completed validation
site.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\textbf{Q1.}
We answer Q1 by specifying an evidence-based AI4SE transformation framework
and method: the path organizes Explore / Consolidation / conditional Scale; the
admission contract distinguishes citable, runnable, and transferable readiness
(stub~$\neq$~ready); and calendar time may prompt review but cannot substitute
for closeout or admission (Section~\ref{sec:path}).

\textbf{Q2.}
Org-A's completed Explore produced a Method/Opportunity/Executable/Evidence
handoff with readiness labels.  The evidence supported Explore closeout and
entry into Consolidation: an observed midpoint recorded 15 requirements,
18 changes, and 8 finished changes, with all 18 changes entering through
\texttt{/sdd}.  It did not support Scale admission because the Executable
main chain remained largely stubbed, the midpoint showed no
Reject/Conditional Verify records and longer-than-expected Align time, and
the AI-orchestration role was still hardening (Section~\ref{sec:org-a}).

\textbf{Q3.}
Org-B's briefing documents a team-level adaptation design in late Explore under
IPD and SAFe-class constraints: P0 runway enablement and an
RR$\rightarrow$IPD layers$\rightarrow$SDD P1 bridge were in focus, while formal
closeout, later-stage delivery, Consolidation, Scale admission, and
ART/Portfolio rewrite remain unclaimed (Section~\ref{sec:org-b-fit}).

Org-A's observed Explore/Consolidation evidence and Org-B's briefing-level
adaptation design together support a bounded claim about the framework's
feasibility and transfer logic; they do not constitute two equivalent
validation sites.  Both organizations plan to use it as the basis for
subsequent scale work, but no organization-wide Scale or platform-measured
return is claimed.  The practical implication is that mid-to-large
organizations can use pilot completion to trigger an evidence review, but
should not treat it as Scale authorization without transfer evidence.
The transferable artifact is therefore the path-plus-admission contract, not a
tool catalog or a completed rollout.
